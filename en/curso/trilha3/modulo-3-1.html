⚖️ Cache vs. Compaction

Caching and compaction seem to compete, but they optimize different things: one changes the price per token, the other in how many tokens you carry. Whoever understands both axes makes the right call — and stops paying for huge context turn after turn.

↔️ Two different axes

Cache optimizes the price per token of the context. Compact optimizes how many tokens you carry. These are two axes that don’t compete in the same place — and that’s why you want both.

Compact = summarize the history into a shorter version; in Claude Code, the command is /compact. It doesn't change the price per token; it changes the size of what you resend.

Legend: the cache only goes down (price); compaction only moves left (quantity). One doesn't do the other's job.

💰 A large cached context is still expensive

The cache discount is misleading. The cache makes per token, but 400k tokens read at 0,1× still equal ~40k of full input per turn — and that every turn, while the context remains this large.

🧮 The calculation that scares you

400,000 tokens × 0.1× = 40,000 full-input-equivalent tokens, paid for every message. A high hit rate (99%) doesn’t change that: the volume is large.

400k read @ 0.1× ≈ 40k-equiv / turn
× every turn of the conversation = a sum that keeps growing

Legend: one tenth of a lot is still a lot. Caching lowers the cost; it doesn't shrink the context.

📊 Read the total, not the field

If input_tokens shows little, but the bill is high; most of it is in cache_read_input_tokens at 0.1×. It’s cheap per token, but the total prompt cost is the sum of the three fields.

3

🧱 The context limit (1M)

Here the cache doesn’t help. It leaves 900k tokens cheap, but they still take up 900k of 1M. Eventually, you can't continue the conversation without compact or /clear. The limit is about quantity — and only compacting/clearing frees up space.

Legend: cheap ≠ small. The 1M limit counts occupied slots, not the price—only compact creates room.

🚧 The common mistake

Rely on the cache to “handle” a context that only keeps growing. It holds down the cost, not the size. Near the limit, the conversation gets stuck—and then it’s /compact or /clear, no more caching.

📉 Compaction numbers

Compacting replaces a single cold write with much lighter turns. Without compacting, you pay for the large context every message. By compacting, you pay for just one write, and all subsequent turns are cheap. See the side-by-side breakdown.

🧠 Quality and latency

Compaction isn’t just about cost. Bloated context scatters attention of the model (the "context rot") — it makes more mistakes and rambles more. And even when read from the cache, 400k take longer than 20k for the first token (higher TTFT). Compacting improves accuracy e speed.

Legend: fewer tokens = concentrated attention + faster first token. Compression is a quality improvement, not just a cost saving.

🎯 When to compact / when NOT to

The guideline is simple. Short conversation → DO NOT compact: you only lose the warm cache without any benefit (that intuition is right). Large context, near the limit, or quality declining → compact: you trade one cold write for much lighter turns.

Legend: the branch green preserves the cache; the branch purple accepts 1 cold write to save on every subsequent turn.

✓ Compact when

  • ✓The context is large, and many turns are still ahead
  • ✓You’re close to the 1M limit
  • ✓Quality has dropped / the model is stalling

✗ Don’t compact when

  • ✗The conversation is short and the cache is already warm
  • ✗Will end soon (there aren’t enough turns left to pay for the cold write)
  • ✗By habit, without checking size or quality

💡 Rule of thumb

Compact with the state (size, limit, quality), not by a clock. And remember: caching and compaction work together — after compaction, the new smaller context is also cached and becomes warm again.
